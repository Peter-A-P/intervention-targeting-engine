Eight ways of choosing, measured on six datasets against random

Every budget picks some people and skips others. Who should it pick?

Retention offers, outreach calls, fraud reviews, follow-up appointments. The money covers a fraction of the population, so somebody chooses the fraction. The usual answer is to rank people by risk and treat the top of the list. Sometimes that works. Sometimes it is worse than picking at random. This page shows both, on real data, and how to tell which case you are in.

The idea in one table

If you could see what your action does to each person, there would be four kinds of people. Only one kind is worth the money.

Kind of personIf you do nothingIf you actWorth the budget?
Persuadablesbad outcomegood outcomeYes. They are the entire return.
Sure thingsgood outcomegood outcomeNo. They were fine anyway.
Lost causesbad outcomebad outcomeNo. Nothing on offer helps.
Sleeping dogsgood outcomebad outcomeNo. Acting does damage.

Ranking by risk sorts on the first column alone. It puts lost causes at the top, because they really are the highest risk, and it cannot see sleeping dogs at all. Persuadables sit in the middle of a risk list and get missed. Uplift modelling tries to sort on the difference between the two columns instead. That difference is what the budget buys, and it can never be observed for any one person, because only one of the two ever happens to them.

How it works

From six public datasets to a budget decision

The rest of this page is the measurement, in the order it happens. Each part starts in plain language, with a picture. Where there is more to say for someone who builds models for a living, it is folded away under For the technical reader, so you can skip it if you only want the idea.

The whole idea in one picture

Five steps. The order matters: the test rows are set aside before any model sees the data, and every method is scored on them once, beside the two baselines.

  1. 1LoadSix public datasets, each checked against a recorded checksum.
  2. 2SplitTraining, validation and test rows on five fixed seeds. The test rows are used once.
  3. 3RankEight ways of choosing who to treat, through one interface.
  4. 4ScoreWhat each ranking buys at a budget, with a 95% interval, against random targeting and ranking by risk.
  5. 5DiagnoseOne outcome model says in advance whether ranking by risk will do.

Ranking by risk is measured like every other method. It is scored beside every uplift model on every dataset, which is how the project found it can be the best choice on one dataset and worse than doing nothing on another.

Could your own data answer this at all?

Most data cannot, and finding out afterwards is expensive. Two very different questions hide behind one word, "enough":

  1. Does the intervention do anything? The easy question, and the one most pilots are sized for.
  2. Is targeting better than picking at random? Much harder, because you now need to tell one group apart from the average rather than from zero.

These are floors, not estimates. They price measuring a ranking you already have, and a real study also has to learn the ranking from the same rows, which costs more. How much better the top group responds is the thing the study exists to discover, so it cannot be known in advance, which is why the answer is a range rather than a number.

The same arithmetic, run on the six datasets above from four summary numbers each and no model at all, gets every one of the six verdicts right: the four that could be ranked on, and the two that could not. That check is a test in the repository, so it cannot quietly stop being true.

What the data proved wrong

Several things the project expected turned out wrong when they were measured. Each is in the repository with its evidence. A few of them:

"Ranking by risk is the wrong thing to do"

The project was built to show it. On 14 million randomised Criteo rows, risk ranking matches every uplift model.

Tuning every model

The grid search took about 89% of every benchmark's compute. Four measurements over six weeks found it chose between near-ties and changed nothing a reader would act on.

A fraud case rigged for uplift

Review was simulated to do least good on the riskiest transactions. Risk ranking still beat every uplift model at every budget.

"Churned" and "retained" gave opposite answers

The same file, labelled two ways. The design and the outcome's direction are now required arguments with no default.

Five rows nobody could reproduce

The fraud loader encoded categories differently in different processes. A rerun from a fresh copy found it, and the table was remeasured.

One approach is written up in full as rejected: tuning every estimator on a validation grid. Read it here.

What this does not do

  • It needs an intervention that already happened to some people and not others. It cannot estimate the effect of an action nobody has taken, and the cheap diagnostic assumes who got it was decided at random.
  • One treatment, yes or no. No continuous or multi-valued treatments, and no online allocation.
  • The fraud case's review effect is simulated. The transactions and the fraud labels are real.
  • Dragonnet is untuned, and on the three largest datasets it saw at most 200,000 training rows. Its column is not a like-for-like comparison with the LightGBM learners.
  • Criteo is a 10% subsample, 1,397,958 of 13,979,592 rows. The fit on the full file is not done.
  • The sensitivity analysis covers three of the five benchmark datasets, and none of the three could fail it.

What to take from it

Risk ranking is neither a trap nor safe

It depends on the data, and the difference can flip the sign of the result. Nobody can guess in advance which case they are in. On ACIC it loses value; on Criteo it matches every uplift model. Both results came out of the same code.

You can tell which case you are in, cheaply

One outcome model, before any of the expensive work. That diagnostic is the most useful thing this project produced, and it was not what the project set out to build.

Most data cannot answer the question

Lenta has 687,029 customers in a real randomised trial and still cannot support a targeting decision. Size alone is not enough, and the calculator above says so before you spend the money.

Every number here has an interval

A result without one is treated as a defect in this codebase. That rule is what kept the conclusions standing through six defects found during the build: every corrected number stayed inside the range already published.

Running it on your own data, in three commands

Check you have the right kind of data

You need an intervention that already happened to some people and not others, and a record of what happened to both: a customer list on its own is not enough. Nothing here, and nothing anywhere, can tell you the effect of an action nobody has ever taken.

Ideally who got it was decided at random. If it was not, the numbers mix what the intervention did with whatever made those people likelier to be chosen. Run with --design observational, the tool prints that caveat above its own table.

If you have no data yet, size the pilot

Use the calculator above, then run a holdout of that size. One command does the arithmetic:

uv run itx power \
  --base-rate 0.10 \
  --relative-effect 0.10 \
  --budget 0.2

It reports the easy threshold and the hard one separately, because sizing for the easy one is the usual mistake.

Spend one model finding out if it is worth continuing

Before fitting anything sophisticated, ask whether risk ranking already does the job on your population. It costs a single model: 

uv run itx diagnose \
  --csv customers.csv \
  --treatment got_offer \
  --outcome churned \
  --features age,tenure,plan \
  --categorical plan \
  --design randomised \
  --outcome-polarity lower-is-better

The last two have no defaults and are never guessed. The same file labelled "churned" and "retained" gave opposite answers until that was fixed.

Only then, run the full benchmark

If the diagnostic says there is room, fit everything and compare it against both baselines with intervals on every number, the way this page does. 

uv run itx benchmark \
  --dataset hillstrom

And pass --no-tune. The tuning search was measured four times and bought nothing.

How to read these numbers

What "outcome bought per head" means

It is measured per person in the whole population, not per person treated. That way a 5% budget and a 40% budget sit on the same axis and can be compared directly. Treating 5% of people very well can still buy less in total than treating 40% adequately.

Why every number has a range after it

The range is a 95% confidence interval: where the answer would likely fall if you ran the whole thing again on fresh data. A method whose range crosses the random line has not been shown to beat random targeting, whatever its headline number says. Reporting the number alone would make several results on this page look far more certain than they are.

This page shows one split, the repository averages five

The results table in the repository averages five random splits of each dataset. This page shows the first of them, because a slider has to move through a single ranking of actual people rather than an average of five different ones. So these numbers sit near the table's and will not equal them. On ACIC at a 10% budget this split gives risk ranking -0.2434 where the five-split average is -0.1971.

Where the numbers come from

The random line is the closed-form expectation: the budget share times the value of treating everybody. The repository's random row is the average of 200 actual random draws, so the two sit close together and differ slightly.

For the technical reader: the choices that are easy to get wrong
  • The test split is touched once. Settings are chosen on a validation split, and the test rows are scored only after that.
  • Two estimates of what a budget buys, side by side. Inverse-probability weighting and a doubly robust estimator are both reported, because a disagreement between them is worth seeing.
  • No defaults where a guess would flip the answer. --design and --outcome-polarity must be given; the same file labelled "churned" and "retained" gave opposite answers until they were.
  • Every input is checked against a committed digest, and every table was regenerated from a fresh copy and compared at zero tolerance. That rerun is what found the fraud loader's encoding defect.
  • The page computes nothing the benchmark could. Every figure is read from JSON written by itx demo build. The only arithmetic in the browser is the sample-size calculator, and a test checks it against the Python it mirrors.

How this was built

I designed this project and made the calls in it: which datasets to use, how to score a ranking, and what to do when a measurement came back against the plan. The call that shaped it most was to treat ranking people by risk, the way this job is usually done, as a baseline to measure beside every uplift model rather than as the thing to beat. It turned out to be the best choice on Criteo and worse than doing nothing on ACIC, which is why itx diagnose exists and why it is the first thing to run. The project set out to show the opposite, and I kept the result. The same rule applied to my own protocol: tuning every model took most of the compute, four measurements said it changed nothing a reader would act on, and I dropped it.

I used an AI coding assistant (Claude Code) for implementation and drafting, which is now standard practice in software, data science and machine learning work. Every number on this page can be reproduced from the repository with one command, and every table there has been rebuilt from a fresh copy and matched at zero tolerance.
